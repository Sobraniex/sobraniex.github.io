Where does your business data go?
01 / Data and AIA clear path from task to data decision

A familiar workday

Where does customer data go when staff use AI?

Say a customer emails your shop to ask when an order will arrive. An employee could ask AI to draft a reply using your delivery policy. It may save a few minutes. Before sharing customer details, decide what the AI needs and where that information will go.

ONE TASK / TWO ROUTES

The same reply can be drafted in two ways.

01

Hosted AI

Staff send the selected text over the internet → the provider processes it → a draft comes back. Copies or logs may remain under that product’s terms.

02

Carefully configured local AI

Staff send only the needed details to a model on company hardware → the draft returns inside the company. Check that connectors, logs, backups and updates do not send the content elsewhere.

What to check with any hosted service

Three separate questions about the same message

The answers depend on the exact product, account, settings and agreement. A business or API account can differ from a personal chat account.

01

Model training

Will the provider use the message to improve later versions of its AI? Turning training off addresses that use, but does not itself erase stored copies.

02

Data retention

Will the prompt, reply or logs be kept, where, by whom and for how long? Retained data may later be used for permitted safety reviews or service support, or become relevant to a legal request or a security incident.

03

Government access

Can an authority require a provider under its jurisdiction to disclose data it still holds or controls? That takes a legal route with scope and limits. It is not automatic access to every chat.

01 / 05

Court and regulator records

Documented incidents, orders and penalties

Each record says what kind of evidence it is: a company’s allegation, a regulator’s preliminary action, a court ruling or a settlement. Some older platform privacy cases predate generative AI and do not establish how AI prompts were handled. These examples show why it is worth checking data use, retention and safeguards for the exact product and account type.

Moonshot / Kimi: Anthropic alleges customer data was routed to Claude

In a report published 10 September 2026, Anthropic alleged that Moonshot silently forwarded some Kimi customer requests to Claude and showed Claude’s answers as Kimi’s. Anthropic said it observed nearly 300,000 relayed requests in one ten-day period; it also said Moonshot saved at least some exchanges to extract model reasoning for training. The requests reportedly included sensitive customer material, including internal code, live access credentials and surveillance footage. These are Anthropic’s findings, not an independent court ruling; the report says Anthropic does not know whether Moonshot told affected customers.

On 22 September, The Information reported, citing people familiar with the matter, that China’s cyberspace regulator was investigating Moonshot and DeepSeek over possible user-data leaks to Anthropic and had interviewed company staff. As of 25 September 2026, the report said the investigation was ongoing and no penalty decision had been made. This reported investigation is not a public finding that a breach occurred.

Anthropic report, 10 Sep 2026The Information, 22 Sep 2026
Z.ai / GLM: Anthropic alleges Claude reasoning was extracted

Anthropic’s September 2026 report attributes a Claude reasoning-extraction campaign to Zhipu, which brands its products internationally as Z.ai. Anthropic says 273 accounts ran a reasoning-extraction pipeline over one ten-day period, and attributes more than 3.4 million exchanges to Zhipu over 17 days in June and July 2026. It says some results were used to train GLM models. This is Anthropic’s account of model-distillation activity, not a court or regulator finding. The report does not make the same claim it made about Moonshot and DeepSeek: that Z.ai secretly routed its own customers’ prompts to Claude.

Anthropic report, Z.ai section
Anthropic: books, training and a $1.5 billion settlement

Authors said Anthropic copied books without permission. In June 2025, a US judge ruled that using the books to train models was fair use, while separately ruling that keeping pirated copies in a permanent library was not fair use. In July 2026, the court approved a $1.5 billion settlement of class claims over those copies. A settlement is not a government fine or an admission that all AI training is unlawful.

2025 court ruling2026 settlement approval
OpenAI: Italy’s €15 million privacy fine was annulled

In December 2024, Italy’s data protection authority fined OpenAI €15 million, citing its findings about the legal basis and transparency for personal data used to train ChatGPT, among other issues. On 18 March 2026, the Rome court annulled the authority’s decision. The fine is therefore not a current final penalty, and the authority’s findings should not be presented as a final court judgment.

Italian authority: decision and update
OpenAI: a court ordered some logs preserved

In May 2025, during copyright litigation, a US court ordered OpenAI to preserve output logs that would otherwise have been deleted. A later court filing ended the broad prospective preservation duty as of 26 September 2025, with limits for some previously preserved records. This was a litigation hold, not a general product rule or a finding that those logs were used for training.

May 2025 court orderLater court filing
DeepSeek: Italy’s regulator disputed the company’s position

In January 2025, DeepSeek’s companies told Italy’s authority they did not operate in Italy and EU data protection law did not apply. The authority said the service was accessible to Italian users, opened an investigation and restricted processing; its formal order later declared specified processing unlawful under GDPR. The published record documents a disagreement and regulatory action, not a court finding that the companies intentionally lied. The order did not impose a financial penalty.

Authority announcementFormal order
Meta: Brazil paused AI training; separate privacy settlements

AI-specific action: in July 2024, Brazil’s data protection authority (ANPD) ordered Meta to pause using public content from its platforms to train generative AI in Brazil. The authority cited preliminary concerns about legal basis, clear notice, users’ ability to object and minors’ data. It set a R$50,000-per-day fine for non-compliance, but that was a threatened penalty, not a fine shown as paid. After Meta appealed, ANPD suspended the pause on 30 August 2024 and allowed training to resume under a compliance plan, including clearer notice, easier objections and restrictions on using minors’ data while oversight continued. This was a preliminary regulatory process, not a final finding that all such training was unlawful.

Separate, older platform privacy matters: in 2024 Meta agreed to a $1.4 billion Texas settlement over allegations that Facebook collected and used Texans’ face geometry without legally required authorization. In 2019 Facebook agreed to a $5 billion FTC settlement over charges that it violated an earlier privacy order and misled users about control of personal information. These matters concern biometric or platform privacy practices from before generative AI; they are not penalties for AI prompts or model training.

Legal requests / jurisdiction matters

What authorities may request

A provider’s location, legal presence and control of the data matter more than the model’s country of origin. A locally run model does not send prompts to its publisher just because the model was developed abroad.

US

US providers

US authorities can use legal process under the Stored Communications Act to seek stored content or account records from a provider under US jurisdiction. The process required depends on the data.

Under the CLOUD Act, the provider may have to produce data it controls even if stored abroad. This does not give the US jurisdiction over every foreign provider; foreign-law conflicts can be argued in court.

18 USC §270318 USC §2713DOJ: limits
CN

Providers in China

China’s Data Security Law permits public security or national security organs to obtain data for lawful national-security or criminal investigations after required approvals. Relevant organisations must cooperate.

The law restricts handing data stored in China to foreign law-enforcement bodies without Chinese approval. It does not grant automatic access to every prompt or directly compel an unrelated EU company with no relevant Chinese presence.

Data Security Law §§35–36Personal Information Law §41

Prepare for the AI Act and protect personal data

The AI Act rolls out by use and risk. GDPR already applies when a workflow processes personal data. Neither law is satisfied by buying a local computer.

What to do now

List the AI tools and tasks staff use. Identify whether you are providing or deploying a system and whether a use is high-risk. Put human review, staff guidance and any required transparency into the workflow. For personal data, record purpose and legal basis, minimise what is sent, limit access and retention, check transfers and assess whether a data protection impact assessment is needed.

Why businesses choose local AI

01

More control over business data

A local model can keep prompts on company systems instead of sending them to an outside AI provider. Secure user access, backups, connected apps, logs and updates too; local hardware alone does not make a setup secure.

02

Costs you can plan around

Buy the equipment up front, then cover its running costs. Local can cost less when your team uses AI regularly; a hosted service can cost less for occasional use.

03

A setup your business can shape

Choose the model, who can use it and when it gets updated. Open models give you options as your needs change; your team remains responsible for updates and access.

Our view: AI will become a regular work tool. Businesses should be able to choose where it runs. Local models are one useful option; some tasks may still fit a hosted service better.
