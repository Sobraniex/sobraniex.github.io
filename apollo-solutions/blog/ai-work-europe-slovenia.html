AI is changing work. Who gets the gains?

What Anthropic heard from Claude users, and the questions we think European and Slovenian businesses should ask next.

The most useful question for us is not simply how much work AI can perform. It is whether better tools give people more useful work, stronger skills and better services—and who receives the value when a task becomes faster.

What the study found

In an April 2026 study, Anthropic analysed open-ended responses from 80,508 people using personal Claude accounts. About one fifth voiced concern that AI could displace work. Concern was higher among people in occupations whose tasks Claude is already observed doing, and among respondents early in their careers.

Many respondents also described productivity gains. Among those who explicitly discussed such gains, 48% described being able to do a broader range of tasks and 40% described working faster. People in both high- and low-paid occupations reported substantial benefits. Yet the respondents who described the greatest speedups were also more likely to worry about losing work. A faster task can feel like an opportunity and a threat at the same time.

What those numbers cannot prove

This was a voluntary survey of Claude users, not a representative survey of workers in Europe or Slovenia. Anthropic used AI to infer some respondents' occupations, career stages and sentiments from their written answers. The findings describe reported experience and concern; they do not measure jobs lost, wages gained or GDP growth. We should treat them as useful signals for questions to investigate, not as a forecast.

What it could mean for Europe

Eurostat reports that 20% of EU enterprises with at least ten people used AI technologies in 2025. That is adoption, not a measure of economic return. Our reading is that Europe will gain most where firms redesign a real process around a useful tool: better product information, faster document handling, more responsive customer service or software that removes repetitive work. Simply adding an AI subscription does not establish that output or wages have improved.

Distribution matters too. If a team can produce more, the gain might become higher pay, more capacity, lower prices, better service or a larger margin. None of those outcomes is automatic. We think European employers should measure both the work that improves and the opportunities people keep to learn, especially in junior roles where routine tasks have often been the starting point for expertise.

A Slovenian starting point

Slovenia's statistical office (SURS) found that 22% of enterprises with at least ten people used AI technologies in 2025. The share was 18% among small enterprises and 72% among large ones. In the same survey, 56% of enterprises said a lack of suitable staff or knowledge hampered digital transformation. The figures do not cover microbusinesses, so they cannot describe every shop or independent practice.

For Slovenia, we see the main economic opportunity in helping smaller teams do work they could not previously afford or organise well, while keeping local language, customer context and quality in view. A shop might improve its catalogue and support workflow; a service firm might make internal knowledge easier to find. Some tasks may suit a hosted system, others a carefully configured local model. The choice should follow the task, data and total cost, rather than a promise that AI will replace a whole job.

How we would approach it

We would start with one repeatable task, record its current time and quality, test a tool with real users, and check the result. We would ask who gains the saved time, who checks mistakes, and how new colleagues will still learn the underlying work. That is a more useful economic test than counting AI accounts. It is also how we think technology can strengthen a business without treating its people as an afterthought.

Sources: Anthropic's 81,000-person study, 22 April 2026; Eurostat enterprise AI adoption, 2025; SURS digital entrepreneurship, 2025. The economic implications and recommendations are Apollo's interpretation, not findings measured in those sources.
